Meta gives Muse a better memory and more persistence

Meta is rolling out specialized models trained using Muse Wonder to Muse and Meta AI, bringing Active Memory and Active Focus to everyone. The headline numbers are a 2.4x reduction in forgetting critical information and a 67% improvement at finishing everyday tasks when something goes wrong.

8 Nov 2026  •  4 min read

Key takeaways

  • Meta announced on November 6, 2026 that specialized versions of its existing models, trained using Muse Wonder, are rolling out to Muse and Meta AI, bringing Active Memory and Active Focus to both.
  • Active Memory helps the assistants remember what matters and recognize when earlier information becomes relevant again. Meta reports a 2.4x reduction in forgetting critical information, and 90% correct use of critical context on its Active Memory Eval, up from 76%.
  • Active Focus keeps Muse working toward a goal when unexpected problems appear. Meta reports a 67% improvement in completing everyday tasks where unexpected issues arise.
  • Meta also reports the current Muse model scores 47% on its Personal Superintelligence Index, up from 38%, but every figure in this article is Meta's own evaluation, with no independent verification published yet.

What happened

Meta announced on November 6, 2026 that it is rolling out specialized versions of the models behind Muse and Meta AI, trained using its most capable model series, Muse Wonder, and that the update brings two of Wonder's signature traits to everyone: Active Memory and Active Focus.

The move follows the pattern Meta set when it introduced Muse Wonder on October 12: the flagship series would first power Muse experiences for subscribers, then be used to improve the models behind the assistants everyone uses. This rollout is that second step. The models being deployed are not Wonder itself, but specialized versions of Meta's existing models, improved through training with Wonder.

Remembering what matters

Active Memory is Meta's answer to the oldest complaint about AI assistants: that they forget. According to the announcement, the models are trained to recognize what type of information is temporary versus permanent, to reason across time, and to distinguish between what is still relevant and what has become stale. In practice, that means an assistant that can pick up a detail from an earlier conversation when it becomes relevant again, instead of asking you to repeat it.

The numbers Meta reports for Muse are striking. The company says the improved models show a 2.4x reduction in forgetting information critical to a user's goals, and on its Active Memory Eval the current model correctly uses critical context in 90% of tasks, compared with 76% for the previous model. Those two figures describe different parts of the problem: retaining the information, and applying it correctly when the moment comes.

Keeping going when things go wrong

Active Focus targets the other familiar failure: an assistant that gives up the moment a task hits a snag. Meta says it trained the models on a variety of bottlenecks drawn from real-world conditions, so they can try harder, recover, or find alternatives on your behalf. In Muse, the company reports a 67% improvement in completing everyday tasks where unexpected issues arise, like a page that will not load or an item that is out of stock.

The same training also covers how the assistant works with you while a task is running. Meta says the specialized models learned to multitask, to judge when to push on independently and when to collaborate live, and to adapt to steers and clarifications in the middle of a task without getting confused. In Meta AI, the improvements are pitched differently: a better thinking partner that breaks complex problems into clear steps, asks sharper follow-up questions, and keeps pace when you change direction mid-conversation.

Personal Superintelligence Index

Meta's internal benchmark for how its assistants perform on personal and professional agentic tasks. The current Muse model scores 47% on it, up from 38% for the previous model, according to Meta. It is a company-designed test, not an industry standard, so treat the score as a progress marker rather than a ranking against other assistants.

What it means for you

If you use Muse or Meta AI, the practical test is continuity. The update should mean fewer repeated explanations, work that survives a change of circumstances, and tasks that recover from obstacles instead of stalling. That is the promise, at least, and it arrives as part of a busy week for Muse: voice conversations and expressive avatars began rolling out in the US on November 5, and Spaces, which turns a description into a custom mini-app inside Muse, launched on November 6.

What is still unclear: Meta has not published a regional rollout schedule, so it is not clear when every user will see the new models, and it has not shared evaluation baselines, sample sizes, or methodology for any of the figures above. There are also no separate metrics for Meta AI, only for Muse. The 2.4x, 67%, and 47% figures are all Meta's own measurements on Meta's own tests.

Warning

Every performance number in this article comes from Meta itself. No outside lab has independently verified the Active Memory Eval, the 67% task-completion improvement, or the Personal Superintelligence Index score. Treat them as vendor claims until third-party testing confirms them.

The honest way to judge the update is not the announcement but the next few weeks of use: whether Muse remembers the thing you told it last Tuesday, and whether it finds another way when the first way fails. If it does both, the numbers will not matter much. If it does neither, they will not matter at all.
